# Publication Abstract — v0.1

## Title

**Before I Knew What to Measure: Following an Intuition Through Behavioral Data, Space Weather, and the Search for What Actually Changed**

## Abstract

This evidence-backed personal investigation examines a narrow methodological question: can intuition be useful as an early signal for anomaly detection without being treated as evidence of causal knowledge? The case began with a retrospectively reported period of unusual fatigue, sleepiness, muscular weakness, reduced exercise capacity, and difficulty sustaining deep work. Because the initial intuition pointed toward solar activity, the investigation reconstructed behavioral traces from Mac and iPhone Screen Time and compared them with selected observed and forecast space-weather records.

The behavioral record supports a meaningful change in device-use pattern during the latter part of August 2026, but it does not establish its physiological cause. Mac engagement fell substantially across the selected comparison windows while category-labelled productive activity declined much less, suggesting a possible reduction in persistence rather than a complete loss of productive capacity. At the same time, geomagnetically active intervals on August 18–19 occurred alongside comparatively high Mac activity, providing an important counterexample to a simple model in which geomagnetic activity alone is sufficient to explain the later state. Timing, proxy quality, retrospective symptom reporting, competing physiological explanations, and the absence of a demonstrated dose-response relationship further constrain causal interpretation.

The central result is therefore methodological rather than biological: **anomaly detection is not causal identification**. Intuition may be useful for generating a hypothesis or directing attention toward an anomaly, but measurement, controls, predefined variables, and falsification must determine what survives. No causal biological effect of space weather is claimed. The case also suggests that digital behavioral traces may become more informative when modeled through duration, composition, fragmentation, timing, and persistence rather than total Screen Time alone.
