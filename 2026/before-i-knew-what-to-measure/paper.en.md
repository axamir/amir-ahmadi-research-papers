# Before I Knew What to Measure

## Following an Intuition Through Behavioral Data, Space Weather, and the Search for What Actually Changed

**Author:** Amir Ahmadi  
**Status:** Working paper  
**Date:** August 2026

---

For almost a week, I stopped feeling like myself.

Not emotionally. Not philosophically.

Physically and cognitively.

My energy dropped sharply. My muscles felt weak. Concentration became difficult. I could not sustain the kind of research and deep work that normally occupies much of my waking time. I stopped going to the gym—not because I had decided to take a break, but because I simply did not feel that I had the energy for it.

And then there was the sleep.

I normally do not sleep much. But suddenly I wanted to sleep constantly.

The pattern was particularly strange.

At around three or four in the morning, I could sometimes feel almost normal again. My mind would become usable. I could read, think, investigate, write.

Then, around nine in the morning, something seemed to change.

Energy fell.

Concentration disappeared.

Sleep became overwhelmingly attractive.

Sometimes I would sleep into the afternoon or evening and still not feel restored.

Then, sometime after midnight, the cycle would begin again.

After several days of this, I started looking for an explanation.

I could not identify an obvious change in my nutrition or routine that seemed proportional to what I was experiencing.

Then an unusual thought appeared.

**What if something outside me had changed?**

I do not know exactly why, but my mind went to the Sun.

So I checked.

And, unfortunately for my objectivity, something really was happening.

---

## The moment intuition becomes dangerous

I had not been following space-weather reports.

I did not know beforehand that there had been significant solar activity.

But when I looked, I discovered that on August 25 the Sun had produced multiple M-class flares, including an approximately M6.9 event. Space-weather agencies were also monitoring CMEs and high-speed solar-wind conditions, with geomagnetic watches issued for the following days.

Later, geomagnetic activity around Earth did increase, reaching minor-storm conditions during parts of the period.

That discovery produced an immediate and very human reaction:

**Maybe I found it.**

And that was exactly the moment I became suspicious of my own reasoning.

Because finding an external event after intuitively deciding to look for one does not establish a connection.

It establishes only two facts:

Something unusual was happening to me.

Something measurable was happening in the space environment.

Everything between those two statements was still missing.

**Coincidence is not causality.**

So instead of trying to prove my intuition correct, I decided to see how quickly I could make it fail.

---

## I had accidentally been collecting data

I did not have a laboratory.

I did not have continuous EEG, cortisol measurements, blood tests, or a research-grade sleep monitor.

But I did have something else.

My devices had been recording parts of my behavior before I ever thought about this hypothesis.

Mac Screen Time.

IPhone Screen Time.

Hourly activity.

Application categories.

Battery activity.

These data obviously do not measure fatigue, cognition, or muscular strength directly.

Screen Time is not a biomarker.

But it can leave a behavioral trace.

And because much of it had been recorded **before the hypothesis existed**, it offered something my memory could not: a partially independent record of what I had actually been doing.

I began reconstructing the days.

And almost immediately, I discovered that I had been looking at the wrong variable.

---

## Twelve hours online can mean two completely different things

At first, total Screen Time seemed like the obvious metric.

If I was exhausted, perhaps I should simply have been using my devices less.

The data did not cooperate.

On August 29, my iPhone Screen Time was only about **4 hours and 13 minutes**.

On August 30, it was approximately **12 hours and 54 minutes**.

That looked terrible for my hypothesis.

How could I describe myself as depleted while spending nearly thirteen hours on my phone?

Then I looked at what those thirteen hours contained.

More than eleven hours were classified as Social and Entertainment.

That distinction matters.

When I am cognitively engaged, I can spend very long periods researching, writing, analyzing, building, reading technical material, or moving between tools with a specific objective.

When I cannot sustain that state, being awake does not necessarily mean being productive.

I can lie in bed.

Scroll.

Open social media.

Look at financial markets.

Jump between pieces of content.

Start something and abandon it.

Remain digitally active without being cognitively engaged.

The comparison with earlier August made this even clearer.

On August 10, I had approximately **16 hours and 20 minutes** of iPhone Screen Time—more than on August 30.

But roughly **9 hours and 25 minutes** of it was categorized as Productivity & Finance.

High Screen Time had occurred in both states.

Its **composition** was radically different.

That produced the first unexpected insight of the investigation:

> **The interesting signal was not how much I used my devices. It was what my mind appeared capable of doing while I was awake.**

Total Screen Time was a poor proxy for cognitive capacity.

Behavioral composition might be more informative.

---

## Then the MacBook told another part of the story

The Mac data added a second dimension.

Compared with the preceding period, my overall Mac engagement dropped substantially during the final part of August.

There were earlier days with roughly eleven, twelve, even thirteen hours of Mac activity.

Toward the period in question, several days fell closer to four, five, or six hours.

This was not proof of fatigue.

But it was consistent with my recollection that something had changed in my ability to remain engaged for long periods.

More interestingly, the decline was not simply equivalent to “no productivity.”

There were still windows in which I worked.

What seemed to change more strongly was my ability to **sustain the state**.

That suggested another possible behavioral variable:

not productivity itself,

but **cognitive persistence**.

Perhaps the anomaly was not:

> I could no longer work.

Perhaps it was:

> I could still enter a productive state, but I could no longer reliably remain there.

That distinction would become important.

---

## Then the data began attacking the solar hypothesis

If geomagnetic activity were responsible for what I was experiencing, a simple prediction should follow:

Periods of comparable or stronger geomagnetic disturbance should tend to coincide with similar deterioration.

So I looked backward.

And I found a problem.

Around August 18–19, geomagnetic activity had already reached approximately Kp 5 / G1 conditions during parts of the period.

Yet my Mac activity on those days was roughly:

**August 18: 11h 02m**

**August 19: 11h 24m**

Those did not look like the behavioral state I was trying to explain.

This was my first useful counterexample.

The simple hypothesis—

> **higher geomagnetic activity → lower functioning**

—was already in trouble.

And there was another timing problem.

The solar activity that attracted my attention occurred around August 25, while the more notable geomagnetic response near Earth developed later.

But the behavioral change I was trying to understand may have begun earlier than the strongest geomagnetic disturbance.

That matters enormously.

A cause cannot conveniently arrive after its effect simply because the narrative is attractive.

So the first serious examination of the data did something I had not expected.

It did not confirm my intuition.

**It began dismantling its explanation.**

---

## A real event is not necessarily a real connection

This became the central distinction of the investigation.

My intuition had led me toward something that actually existed.

There really had been notable solar activity.

There really had been CMEs and changing solar-wind conditions.

There really had been subsequent geomagnetic disturbance.

But:

> **The existence of an environmental event is evidence that the event occurred—not evidence that it affected me.**

This sounds obvious when written explicitly.

It is much less obvious when you have just searched for something because of an intuition and discovered that it is actually there.

The human mind is extraordinarily good at closing that gap.

“I felt something.”

“I suspected X.”

“I checked X.”

“X was happening.”

Therefore:

“X caused what I felt.”

That final step can happen almost invisibly.

And perhaps the most important decision in this entire investigation was refusing to take it.

---

## Trying to kill the hypothesis

Instead of asking:

**What evidence supports my intuition?**

I began asking:

**What evidence should exist if my intuition is wrong?**

That changes everything.

If geomagnetic activity matters, what happens on active days when I feel completely normal?

If I experience the same behavioral anomaly during geomagnetically quiet periods, what happens to the hypothesis?

If a stronger event occurs with no corresponding behavioral change, does that contradict a dose-response relationship?

What if Kp is simply the wrong variable?

Could solar-wind speed matter?

Density?

IMF Bz?

Dst?

And here another danger appears.

If Kp does not work, I can try Bz.

If Bz does not work, I can try solar-wind density.

Then speed.

Then pressure.

Then different delays.

Six hours.

Twelve.

Seventeen.

Twenty-four.

Eventually, with enough variables and enough temporal flexibility, something may appear correlated.

That would not necessarily be discovery.

It could simply be **confirmation hunting with more sophisticated tools**.

So any continuation of this experiment needs predefined variables and predefined temporal windows.

Otherwise the hypothesis becomes impossible to falsify.

And an unfalsifiable intuition is no longer an investigation.

It is a belief.

---

## The competing explanation I cannot ignore

There is another possibility.

Perhaps the Sun has absolutely nothing to do with this.

The symptoms themselves are nonspecific:

fatigue,

sleepiness,

muscular weakness,

reduced exercise capacity,

difficulty concentrating,

and a substantial change from my normal sleep-wake behavior.

Many ordinary physiological explanations could produce such a state.

An infection or post-viral period.

Sleep or circadian disruption.

Dehydration.

Anemia or iron-related issues.

Glucose or electrolyte abnormalities.

Thyroid-related problems.

Other temporary physiological changes.

At the moment, I do not have the physiological measurements necessary to distinguish among them.

And that matters.

A strange explanation does not become more probable simply because it is interesting.

Common explanations retain higher prior probability until evidence moves us elsewhere.

So the medical question and the research question have to remain separate.

I can investigate the pattern without assuming its cause.

---

## Maybe I misunderstood what intuition was doing

This is where the investigation became more interesting to me than the original solar hypothesis.

What if intuition was not identifying the cause at all?

What if it was doing something more primitive—and perhaps more useful?

Consider two different cognitive operations:

**Anomaly detection**

and

**causal identification.**

They are not the same problem.

A biological system receives an enormous number of signals that never become explicit conscious thoughts: sleep pressure, light, temperature, hormonal state, muscle state, environmental rhythm, routine, social signals, sound, stress, interoceptive information, and countless other weak inputs.

It is conceivable that the nervous system can detect:

**something is different**

without correctly determining:

**this is why.**

In other words:

> **Anomaly Detection ≠ Causal Identification**

Perhaps intuition is sometimes better at the first than the second.

That would explain something important about this experience.

My intuition may have been useful even if its proposed explanation was wrong.

It noticed—or at least accompanied—the perception that something had changed.

Then it generated a search direction.

That direction happened to contain a real event.

The discovery of that event created a tempting causal story.

And the data then forced the story back open.

The sequence became:

**Experience → intuition → hypothesis → external search → discovery → attempted falsification → uncertainty**

That last word matters.

**Uncertainty.**

Not failure.

---

## What the first control taught me

One line captures the turning point:

> **My intuition survived the discovery. It did not survive the first control.**

But even that is incomplete.

Because the failure of the simple explanation did not make the intuition useless.

It changed the question.

The question was no longer:

**Was my intuition right about the Sun?**

It became:

**What exactly is intuition good for?**

Maybe we ask too much of it.

Perhaps intuition should not be treated as a mechanism for knowing.

Perhaps its value lies elsewhere.

It may generate hypotheses.

It may flag anomalies.

It may direct attention toward places we would not otherwise investigate.

It may reduce an enormous search space into a manageable question.

And then its job should end.

Measurement must take over.

---

## The experiment I would run next

The current evidence is retrospective and messy.

So it cannot answer the causal question.

But it can generate a better experiment.

For the next phase, I would record my state without looking at space-weather conditions.

Each day would include simple predefined measures:

energy,

ability to concentrate,

sleep timing,

subjective muscular strength,

exercise capacity,

and ability to sustain deep work.

Behavioral traces from the Mac and iPhone could provide additional independent proxies.

Only afterward would those observations be compared against predefined environmental variables such as Kp, Dst, solar-wind speed, solar-wind density, and IMF Bz.

Temporal relationships would also need to be fixed beforehand—for example 0, 6, 12 and 24-hour windows—rather than selected after seeing which one produces the nicest correlation.

Most importantly, quiet periods and stronger control events would remain in the dataset.

The purpose would not be to find a correlation.

The purpose would be to make the hypothesis **fail as easily as possible**.

If nothing survives, that is a result.

If a weak association survives, it becomes a signal worth observing—not a conclusion.

If the pattern repeats prospectively, under blinded observation, across multiple independent events, then the question becomes more interesting.

And only then.

---

## There is another dataset hiding inside this experiment

Even if the Space Weather hypothesis eventually disappears completely, I think something useful has already emerged.

Our digital behavior may contain subtle traces of cognitive state that total Screen Time obscures.

Six hours of focused research and six hours of fragmented scrolling both appear as six hours on a dashboard.

But they represent very different mental states.

A better behavioral model might consider:

**duration + composition + fragmentation + timing + persistence.**

Not simply:

**hours online.**

In my own data, the transition from sustained computer activity toward more fragmented or passive digital behavior may prove more informative than total device usage.

That possibility deserves investigation independently of anything happening on the Sun.

---

## So, did I feel a solar storm?

I do not know.

And based on the evidence I currently have, I cannot responsibly say that I did.

In fact, some of the evidence argues against the simplest version of that explanation.

There were geomagnetically active periods when my behavior appeared relatively normal.

The timing is imperfect.

There is no clear dose-response relationship.

There are numerous competing physiological explanations.

And Screen Time itself is only a behavioral proxy.

But that does not make the investigation disappointing.

Quite the opposite.

I began with a strange bodily experience.

My intuition pointed toward the Sun.

The Sun happened to be active.

For a moment, that felt like an answer.

Then the data turned the answer back into a question.

And perhaps that is what good inquiry is supposed to do.

---

## Before I knew what to measure

We often talk about intuition as though there are only two possibilities.

Either it is mysterious wisdom that somehow knows the truth before reason catches up,

or it is unreliable noise that rational people should ignore.

I suspect that distinction may be too simple.

Maybe intuition sometimes performs a different function.

Maybe it notices discrepancies before conscious reasoning has constructed a model for them.

Maybe it does not say:

**This is true.**

Maybe it says:

**Something here deserves attention.**

That would make intuition neither evidence nor magic.

It would make it something closer to a **signal generator**.

A mechanism for deciding where measurement might begin.

And that changes how I think about the strange week that led me to look at the Sun.

I still do not know what caused the change I experienced.

I do not know whether the environmental coincidence will survive a better experiment.

It may disappear completely.

In some ways, I hope the experiment is strong enough to make that possible.

Because the question I am left with is now more interesting than the answer I initially went looking for:

> **What does the body notice before the conscious mind knows what to look for?**

And perhaps the lesson is simpler:

> **Maybe intuition is not a way of knowing.**  
> **Maybe it is a way of noticing where knowing should begin.**
